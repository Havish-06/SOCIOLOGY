# Common Answers: what all four of us should say

The professor may ask each of us, separately and on paper, basic questions about the project. This sheet gives
**one shared answer** to each question, taken from the slides and the project documents. Read it, and use these
words and examples so all four answers match. Keep the answers short: a definition, then one example from our
cases.

---

## A. The project in one breath

**1. What is your project about?**
We studied 24 Indian stand-up comedy controversies (2018–2026). We ask what the jokes were about, and why one
controversy ends with only online anger while another ends with police cases, threats or attacks.

**2. What is your main research question?**
What topics are involved when Indian stand-up comedians come under public scrutiny, and when a joke becomes a
controversy, why does it end so differently: online criticism only, legal action, extra-legal action, or both?

**3. What are your three sub-questions?**
- **SQ1, Topic:** what were the jokes about, were they aimed at the powerful or the weak, and does the style
  (genre) matter?
- **SQ2, Context:** how did the clip get out (posted by the comedian or dug up by someone else), and does losing
  the context matter?
- **SQ3, Who reacts and what happens:** who turned the anger into action, through which route, was the comedian
  stigmatised, and was something "sacred" said to be insulted?

**4. What is your main answer?**
The topic alone does not decide the ending. What matters is whether **someone with power has a reason and a tool
to act**: a ruling party (when its leader is mocked), a group the law protects, or a group whose sacred was
touched. Who acts then decides the route, and **the process itself is the punishment**: there were no convictions.

**5. What are the four outcomes?**
| Outcome | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Online only | Criticism online, nothing more | Kapil Sharma on Atlee |
| Legal | FIR, police complaint, court case, a commission's summons | Pranit More 2026 (NCW summons, FIRs) |
| Extra-legal | Outside the law: threats, attacks, forced apologies or cancellations | Pranit More 2025 (attacked in Solapur) |
| Both | Legal and extra-legal together | Kunal Kamra 2025 (FIRs and the venue smashed) |

"Escalated" means anything beyond online only. 19 of our 24 cases escalated.

## B. Key concepts (the most likely questions)

**6. What is dark comedy in your paper?**
A joke about something **grave or painful** (death, violence, rape, disease, disability, tragedy, the sacred, a
low position in a hierarchy) where **the laugh comes from that pain itself**, not from just mentioning it
(Willinger et al., 2017). Examples: Madhur Virli's rape joke; Neville Shah's caste and disability joke.
- **Dark is not the same as offensive.** A political insult can be offensive without being dark.
- **Our finding:** dark comedy is not automatically riskier. Of 9 dark jokes, 3 stayed online only. Dark jokes went
  to commissions and courts, or nowhere; they never led to street action alone.

**7. What is genre, and how is it different from topic?**
**Topic = what** the joke is about (politics, religion, caste, sex). **Genre = how** it is told (its style).
Our genres: dark comedy, satire (mocking the powerful to criticise them), roast (mocking one particular person),
crude or shock (vulgarity), observational (everyday life). Topic is our main comparison; genre is secondary.

**8. What is stigma?**
From **Goffman (1963)**: a person becomes **"tainted"** in society's eyes. People stop thinking "they told a bad
joke" and start thinking "they are a bad person" (for example, being called "anti-national", banned or having shows
cancelled). **Link & Phelan (2001)** describe it as labelling, then separation, then loss of status, then
discrimination, and add that it needs **power** behind it.

**9. What is your stigma ladder?**
Every case is scored on how far the comedian was marked, from 0 to 4 (adapted from Link & Phelan, 2001):
| Step | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | No stigma, just disagreement |
| 1 | Labelling and stereotyping ("insults Shivaji", "anti-national") |
| 2 | Separation: boycott calls, "ban him" |
| 3 | Status loss: shows cancelled, venues lost |
| 4 | Discrimination: bans, arrests, violence |

**Finding:** step 4 was reached in 8 cases (all 7 "both" cases and Pranit More 2025).

**10. What is courtesy stigma?**
Also from Goffman: **people connected to the stigmatised person are punished too**. Examples: the venue where Kamra
performed was vandalised; after Pranit More's 2026 show, an audience member lost his job; organisers face threats.
**Finding:** it reached venues, organisers, co-performers or audiences in 11 cases.

**11. What is "the sacred" (Durkheim)?**
From **Durkheim (1912)**: every group has things it sets apart as untouchable. Insulting them feels like an attack
on the whole group, not just an opinion. It is not only gods. **Our fixed list** (decided before we compared
outcomes): gods and religious symbols; regional heroes (Shivaji); Ambedkar; the nation; the family and parents;
and collective trauma (for example, the Kashmiri Pandit exodus).
- We always ask **"whose sacred?"**: another group's, or the comedian's own group's?
- **Finding:** in India the sacred works as a **legal weapon** (Nair, 2023, "the politics of hurt sentiments").
  Hurt-sentiment and similar laws protect it, so touching it gives groups a ready-made legal route: 6 of the 7
  "both" cases touched the sacred, and none of the 4 street-only cases did.
- **But it is one way into the law, not the only one:** 5 cases with nothing sacred in the joke still went legal,
  through laws on women, caste or misrepresentation. And it is not enough on its own: Sachdeva and Mohanraj touched
  the family and stayed online, because nobody with standing took them up. The sacred is coded **from the joke
  only**, not from what objectors claimed.

**12. What is context collapse?**
In a comedy club, the audience chose to be there and knows it is a joke; that is the context. Online, the clip
reaches **everyone**, including people who never signed up for edgy jokes, so the context "collapses" (Marwick &
boyd, 2011). Example: on *India's Got Latent*, the panel mocked the question at once, but the viral clip cut that
reaction.
- **Finding:** context collapse explains **why anger starts, not how far it goes**. Restoring the full context
  would not have changed most outcomes (Bharti Singh got an FIR although no community was named).

**13. How do you treat context?**
We watched the **full performances** (67 transcripts), not just the viral clips, and compared the two. We also
recorded how the clip got out (collusion or collision) and when (the timing, or "wave").

**14. What are collusion and collision?**
The two ways a clip escapes the room (Davis & Jurgenson, 2014):
- **Collusion:** the comedian put it out (Pranit More posted the 2026 clip himself).
- **Collision:** someone else dug it up or spread it, often later and with an angry caption (Agrima Joshua's 2019
  joke resurfaced in 2020; Bharti Singh's clip was re-titled "Sikh beard").

**Finding:** it matters little. Escalation was 12 of 15 for collusion and 6 of 8 for collision. Collision changes
**who frames** the joke.

**15. What are punching up and punching down?**
- **Punching up:** mocking someone more powerful (Kamra on Deputy CM Eknath Shinde).
- **Punching down:** mocking someone weaker or disadvantaged (Neville Shah on Dalits and disabled people).
- Judged against the **local** hierarchy (caste, religion, state power).

**Finding:** punching up is riskier. None of the 10 "up" jokes stayed online; 1 of 5 "down" jokes did.

**16. What is a mobilising actor?**
**Whoever turns anger into action**: files the police complaint or leads the protest (a party worker, a religious
group, the National Commission for Women). Anger alone does nothing; someone has to act.

**17. What is labelling (Becker)?**
Something becomes "deviant" because people **successfully label** it so, not because of the act itself. The same
joke can be "just a joke" or "an insult to the nation", depending on whose label sticks.

**18. What is benign-violation theory?**
A theory of why jokes are funny (McGraw & Warren, 2010): something feels **wrong** but also **safe**. A club makes
edgy jokes feel safe; once the clip goes viral, the "safe" part disappears and only the "wrong" part remains.

**19. What does "the process is the punishment" mean?**
From **Feeley (1979)**. The real punishment is not a court verdict but the process: FIRs in several states,
summons, police-station visits, jail before bail (Munawar Faruqui spent about a month inside), cancelled shows.
**No case ended in a conviction.**

## C. Method

**20. What is your method?**
A **structured, focused comparison** (George & Bennett, 2005): each of the 24 cases was analysed with **the same
template and the same questions**, then compared side by side.
- **Structured:** the same questions for every case.
- **Focused:** only on the parts that answer our research questions.

**21. How did you choose the cases?**
On purpose, not at random: from 45 candidates, chosen for **variety** (topic, genre, outcome, region, year) and to
make **matched pairs** (same topic, different ending), for example Virli vs Mohanraj and Katikala vs Sarat.

**22. What evidence did you use?**
Two kinds: **news reports** (for what happened) and **the clips themselves**, with 67 transcripts (for what the
joke actually said). Each fact is marked confirmed, reported or disputed.

**23. How did you check the analysis?**
- A **blind re-analysis**.
- Each **case owner watched the clips** and checked their case.
- **144 key facts** were double-checked by a second person (123 confirmed, 21 settled by us), plus 2 spot-checks
  per case.

**24. What are the three danger signs (your main finding)?**
Judged **from the joke alone, before anyone reacted**, so the explanation is not circular:
1. **Mocks a ruler** (a leader whose party holds power): 7 of 7 escalated
2. **Targets a defended group** (one with an official protector in law: women via the NCW, Dalits via the SC/ST
   Act): 5 of 6
3. **Touches another group's sacred:** 4 of 5

Joking about one's **own** group's sacred: 0 of 2 escalated.

**25. How strong is that finding? (say it honestly)**
19 of 24 cases escalated anyway, so "19 of 24 predicted" alone proves nothing. The fair comparison is **with a
sign, 16 of 18 escalated; without a sign, 3 of 6**. The signs are **close to sufficient, but not necessary**.

**26. Who acts, and what route follows?**
| Who acted | Route |
|---|---|
| The party ruling that state | Police and street together (both) |
| A party with no power in that state | Street only |
| A commission (NCW) | Legal |
| Nobody with standing | Fades within about a week |

Example: **Katikala vs Sarat**, same weeks, both mocking Andhra politics. Katikala mocked the ruling party and was
arrested; Sarat performed in Bengaluru and got a stage invasion and a forced apology, but no police case.

## D. Limits and changes

**27. Why only controversies? What is your biggest limit?**
All 24 cases are controversies, so we compare controversies with **each other**. We **cannot say which topics are
risky in general** (selecting on the outcome; Geddes). The numbers are also small, so these are patterns, not
statistics.

**28. What would you do next?**
Add 6–8 jokes with the same danger signs **where nothing happened** (negative cases; Mahoney & Goertz); have two of
us recheck the danger signs blind; and follow the court cases.

**29. What changed from your proposal, and why?**
Only the **method** changed, not the puzzle. We first planned to count YouTube comments; we moved to studying each
case in depth, because the question "why does it end differently?" needs the full story of each case: who acted,
through which route, and what happened. The question was also reworded from "which topics bring comedians under
scrutiny" to "what topics are involved", because our data cannot rank topics by risk. The comment audit is kept
for later.

**30. Apologies: do they help?**
Forced apologies **ended street episodes** quickly (Faruqui 2024 within a day; Sarat on stage). Once a legal
process had started, **an apology never stopped it**.

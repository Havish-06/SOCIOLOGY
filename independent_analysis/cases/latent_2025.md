## Summary

On *India's Got Latent*, Samay Raina's YouTube talent and roast show, guest judge Ranveer Allahbadia asked a contestant whether he would rather watch his parents have sex every day for the rest of his life, or join in once and stop it forever. The episode was recorded in Mumbai on 14 November 2024 and released on 8 February 2025. Third-party Shorts clips of the question spread on 9–10 February 2025. Within about 48 hours there was a lawyers' complaint in Mumbai, a Guwahati FIR announced by Assam's Chief Minister, a Maharashtra Cyber FIR against about 30 people who had appeared on the show, and National Commission for Women (NCW) summonses. YouTube also removed the episode after a government notice. Raina deleted every episode of the show (12 February). Gujarat dates of his tour disappeared from sale, which the VHP claimed as its doing. The Supreme Court gave Allahbadia protection from arrest (18 February) while calling the remark "dirty" and "perverted". Raina was questioned by Maharashtra Cyber on 24 March 2025. The legal process ran for 18 months. It ended on 14 August 2026, when the Supreme Court closed or quashed the remaining proceedings, though reports disagree on exactly what was quashed. The outcome was **both** legal and extra-legal. The key mechanism was a crude sexual joke about **parents**, a fixed sacred object, taken up at once by state actors (two state police forces, a chief minister and statutory commissions) and turned into a long process that worked as the punishment.

## E. Reaction
- Who objected and their framing words: Assam CM Himanta Biswa Sarma ("promoting obscenity and engaging in sexually explicit and vulgar discussion" [6]); Maharashtra CM Devendra Fadnavis (freedom of speech ends "where it encroaches on others' freedom" [2]); NCW ("derogatory remarks", "obscene" [9]); the Supreme Court bench ("dirty mind", "perverted", parents and sisters "ashamed" [15, 16]); VHP Gujarat ("public outrage", "refrain from organising events of such people" [13]); lawyers' complaint ("extremely abusive language", disrespect to women [3, 4]).
- Norm said to be broken: **family_parents; decency_obscenity; women_dignity** (the women's commissions brought in the last one).
- Humour function: for the original audience, **differentiation/identification** (an in-group of fans who enjoy crude, boundary-pushing banter). As framed by objectors, **enforcement in reverse**: the joke was seen as an attack on the family and on Indian decency, so the objectors themselves became the enforcers.
- Who defended the comedian: some comedians and celebrities (for example, the rapper Badshah backed Raina [search result, not opened]); internet-freedom groups criticised the regulation push. No organisational defence of the remark itself; most defenders said the remark was bad but the state response was excessive.
- Media framing: broken by entertainment and viral-news sites (FPJ, News18-type outlets), then by national news through the FIR and CM angle. Dominant frame in week 1: **obscenity / hurt to parents and family**. In the legal phase: **legal overreach vs. obscenity** (SC remarks, regulation debate). ## F. Outcome
- Outcome type: **both**.
- Legal actions against the comedian(s):
  - Complaint to Mumbai Police Commissioner and a women's commission, 10 February (lawyers Ashish Rai and Pankaj Mishra; they did not claim to speak for a group) [3, 4]. Khar police inquiry; statements recorded [4].
  - FIR, Guwahati (Crime Branch / cyber PS), 10 February. Sections reported inconsistently: BNS 79, 95, 294, 296, plus the IT Act and Cinematograph Act are mentioned across sources [1, 5, 6]. Legal basis: **obscenity**; women's modesty (BNS 79).
  - FIR, Maharashtra Cyber, 10/11 February, IT Act (obscenity, section 67), against about 30 people; summonses [7, 8].
  - FIR in Jaipur (against Allahbadia) [15].
  - NCW summons; Maharashtra State Commission for Women takes cognizance [9, 10].
  - Government notice leading to the YouTube takedown of the episode [13].
  - Supreme Court: interim protection with conditions (passport deposit, show ban) for Allahbadia; later relaxed. Proceedings closed in August 2026 (scope disputed) [15, 21, 22].
  - **Administrative**: Raina says he cannot obtain permission for Ahmedabad shows (September 2026) [24]. Reported only.
- Extra-legal actions: Gujarat shows withdrawn after VHP pressure (the VHP claimed it; organisers did not comment) [13, 14]; death threats against Allahbadia and threats against Makhija; Karni Sena threats leading to her IIFA drop; a ₹1 lakh "bounty" announced by a Gwalior outfit [2; reported]. Allahbadia's apology came under heavy pressure (a pressured apology; he is not the coded comedian).
- Highest stigma step: **4** (discrimination: criminal FIRs in three states; passport and show bans on Allahbadia; reported de facto ban on Raina in Ahmedabad). No arrest.
- Courtesy stigma: **yes**. About 30 past guests of all episodes were booked or summoned by Maharashtra Cyber (for example, Raghu Ram was questioned) [7, 8]. Co-panellists were named in the FIRs. Makhija lost an event appearance. Gujarat organisers withdrew shows.
- How it ended: Raina's show returned in June 2026 on Netflix and YouTube, and his special *Still Alive* (April 2026) was a record hit [25]. The Supreme Court closed the cases in August 2026 (scope disputed).
- Winners and losers: losers in the short term were Raina (reported ₹8 crore losses; anxiety [26]), Allahbadia (sponsorships, a temporary show ban) and the co-panellists. Winners: state actors who displayed moral leadership (the Assam CM, the women's commissions); the government's regulatory agenda gained momentum. In the long term Raina gained: his comeback material and season 2 audiences were larger than before.

## G. Comedian's response
- Response (Raina): **combination**. Deletion of all episodes and a statement of cooperation (12 February 2025); regret expressed to the cyber police (24 March 2025, reported); later a narrative of victimhood and defiance in *Still Alive* (2026).
- When: within 2 days of the viral spread, after the FIRs.
- Did it change the course? **Unclear.** The deletion did not prevent further summonses or the SC proceedings. It may have limited fresh complaints, and the SC later barred new FIRs anyway.
- Comedian's own narrative: at first *contrition*; later *blames clipping / blames Allahbadia*. Wikipedia reports that in *Still Alive* he said Allahbadia asked the question eight times and only one take was kept in the edit [2; reported only].

- **Best explanation of this outcome:** The remark violated a sacred object that almost every Indian public shares (parents and the family), so there was no constituency willing to defend it. That allowed state actors to act directly and quickly. A chief minister announced an FIR, the Maharashtra cyber police booked everyone who had appeared on the show, and statutory commissions summoned the creators. The decisive mechanism was therefore **sacred-object violation → state-led moral panic → law as process-punishment**, with context collapse (fan show to Shorts) as the trigger. Extra-legal action (Gujarat cancellations, threats) rode on the state action and did not replace it.
- **Topic:** family and parents (sacred), with sexual crudeness. The shock genre made the joke easy to clip: a single sentence carries the whole offence and needs no setup.
- **Rival explanations ruled out, and why:**
  - *Fame alone*: fame increased visibility. But other equally famous creators' crude content did not draw FIRs; the parents topic did.
  - *Election*: the Delhi result came on 8 February, and no state was in campaign mode in Assam or Maharashtra. Weak.
  - *Religion or caste of the comedians*: no communal framing appears in the sources.
  - *Platform*: partly relevant. The members-first release and third-party clipping mattered, but the content itself would have been actionable on any platform.
